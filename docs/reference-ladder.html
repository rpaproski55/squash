2.0E · Beginner

Learn the game itself

Everything is new: the rules, the gear, how the ball behaves, and how to share a small court safely with another player. Nothing fancy — just get rallies started.

Fundamentals to own

Shots unlocked

Basic serveStraight driveAnything to the front wall

The trap

Learning on a pro ball. A cold double-dot barely bounces — the e-Lessons are blunt that learning "the hard way" just makes the journey longer for no reason.

Drill from the source

Just rally. Court time with a livelier ball (single yellow dot or progress ball), focusing on clean contact and keeping rallies alive.

Source: E-level lessons — rules, equipment & interference

2.5D

Grip & stroke — fix it now, not later

Two focuses only at D level: movement and technique. The grip and swing you build here either carry you up the ladder or cost you months to unlearn later.

Fundamentals to own

Shots unlocked

Straight drives, both sidesBackhand driveHigh, deep length

The trap

Hitting as hard as you can. Hard-hitters at D/C never learn to feel the ball or vary pace — and hitting winners off other D players teaches habits a good C or B will retrieve and punish.

Drill from the source

40 straight backhands. Solo: hit consecutive backhand drives behind the service box, within a metre of the sidewall, aiming ~1½ ft above the service line. Miss one, start the count again. Then the same on the forehand.

Source: The squash grip · How to hit the ball · Training tips for D players

3.0D+

Length, the T, and a simple plan

Strategy starts here — but it stays simple. Good length, back corners, recover to the T. You win by giving and taking more than your opponent, not by hitting winner after winner.

Fundamentals to own

Shots unlocked

Length gameFirst drop shotsLob to reset

The trap

Playing short too early. Commandment 9: don't play short until you've first played length. Short balls hit from a weak position just open the court for your opponent.

Drill from the source

Ghosting + length rallying. Ghost T-to-corners without a ball to groove recovery, then rally with one rule: every shot must land behind the service box before you're allowed a drop.

Source: The Ten Commandments of Squash · More on court strategy

3.5C
● You are here

Watch, anticipate, stay patient

Your level. The jump at C is reading the game: watching the ball and your opponent's preparation, anticipating the likely shot, and resisting the urge to force winners that better players will simply retrieve.

Fundamentals to own — check these honestly

Shots unlocked

Volley lengthStraight dropLobVaried-pace drivesWorking boast

The trap

Expect a dip when you fix technique. The source is explicit: your game will get temporarily worse when you change grip, stroke or strategy — you can lose for months to players you used to beat. They've perfected an imperfect game; you're building a perfect one. Don't abandon the fix.

Drill from the source

Two-shot anticipation. In rallies, silently name your opponent's two most likely shots from each position before they hit — then check your read. Pair it with the hop on every opponent swing until it's automatic.

Source: General tips for C players — watch ball or opponent?

4.0C+
Your next rung

Keep it straight, keep it tight

The defining idea of 4.0: roughly 90% of pro squash is tight straight drives, volleys and straight drops — because the tight straight shot is the only one that can't be attacked. Everything else is seasoning.

Fundamentals to own

Shots unlocked

Lob / hammer / body servesSkid boastTrickle boastHeld & delayed shotsVolley drop

The trap

Going for the volley winner every time. Commandment 7: volley to length first, keep them at full stretch — the short volley is for the opportunity the length volleys create.

Drill from the source

Boast/drive deception drill. Two players: the front player shows one shot and plays another — show drop, play lob; show straight, play cross. It builds your disguise and your reading at the same time.

Source: Tips for 4.0 players · Service types · Boast types

4.5B

Train how you want to play

At B level the separator stops being shots and becomes training quality. Specific, T-centred practice, harder than your matches — and the discipline to rest.

Fundamentals to own

Shots unlocked

Backwall boastVolley boastCorkscrew (rare)Full pace range

The trap

More hours instead of better hours. Grinding daily court time leads to mental and physical fatigue that actively hurts your game — the B lesson and the training-cycle lesson both make the same point.

Drill from the source

Target ghosting. Ghost T-to-corners at match intensity with a target zone called for each corner — every rep ends back on the T, racquet up.

Source: Training tips for the B player (Paul Lord) · The backwall boast

5.0B+

Consistency, patience, the lob

At 5.0+ everyone gets to almost everything. Shots that were winners at C and B come back — so the game becomes about quality, automatic shot selection, and not beating yourself.

Fundamentals to own

Shots unlocked

Disguised lobFull shot matrix under pressure

The trap

Impatience. Named directly by 5.5 players describing what held them back at 5.0: trying to match a stronger opponent's pressure instead of trusting depth and waiting.

Drill from the source

Drills over matches. Shift your week toward drilling for consistency — the 5.0 lesson credits exactly that shift for becoming competitive with 5.5s.

5.5A

Command of pace, trained in cycles

The A-player marker from pro coach Nathan Dugan: there is always more than one way to hit a ball into the same corner — and you can use all of them, at different paces, under pressure.

Fundamentals to own

Shots unlocked

Every shot, every paceDeception as default

The trap

Training flat-out, all the time. Playing every day for hours doesn't get you to the top faster — continuous play produces the fatigue that stalls A-level progress. The cycle is the skill.

Drill from the source

One corner, three paces. Feed to one back corner; hit the same target hard, medium and soft in rotation, without the landing spot moving.

Source: How to optimize your training cycle · pace advice via the 4.0 tips page

6.0Pro

Attrition: pace & tightness break people

Pro squash, per the source: not the odd great shot. Fitness, anticipation and court awareness so high that rallies become attritional — cautious early, explosive the moment weakness shows.

What the pro game actually is

Why it's on your ladder

Every rung below is a piece of this: the D-level stroke, the D+ length game, C-level anticipation, C+ tightness, B-level training quality, B+ patience, A-level pace control. The pro game is the same fundamentals with nothing left loose.

Watch for

The takeaway for a 3.5: pros win with the straight, tight, patient game you're building now — the highlight shots are the last 10%, not the foundation.

Source: What is the Pro's strategy?

The four ingredients of every correct hit

From the D-level "How to hit the ball" lesson — the source says 95% of the difference between your game and a pro's traces back to one of these four. They apply at every rung of the ladder.

01

Wrist cocked

Racquet and forearm at 90°, wrist turned back toward your body. Swing releases the wrist so it finishes pointing away in the follow-through.

02

Racquet up at the T

Above waist level while you wait. You're ready to volley instantly, and switching to backhand is just a small shoulder rotation — no time wasted lifting a dropped racquet.

03

Perpendicular to the wall

Square up: lead foot perpendicular to the sidewall for straight shots. Face the front wall and cross-courts are the only shots your body can play.

04

Bend & lunge

Never hit standing fully upright. The lunge gets you low enough to lift the ball deep — and from that one position you can play drive, drop, lob or boast, so your opponent has to guess.

Where good length lives

The geometry behind half the ladder: own the T, and make the ball die in the back corners.

The court, from above

Good length: hit firmly at the service line on the front wall so the ball lands near the back of the service box and its second bounce dies in the shaded corners. From the T (yellow), most of the court is one stride away — that's why every level from 3.0 up is built on getting back there.

The Ten Commandments — the 3.0 checklist that never expires

  1. Hit to a good length — firmly at the service line, land near the back of the box
  2. Aim to the back corner — the only way to hold the T
  3. Keep it close to the sidewall — the straight shot is the better shot
  4. Recover to the T — 60% of shots are one stride from there
  5. Watch the ball at all times — onto both racquets, even behind you
  6. Make your opponent run — play away from them, up and down the walls
  7. Volley whenever possible — to length first; short only on the real chance
  8. Delay shots — wrong-foot them; stops and starts cause fatigue
  9. Go for short shots — but only after length has earned them
  10. Change the pace — keeps them guessing, and buys you recovery time

Match-play summary from the same source: aim for good length, play some drops, vary the pace, and study your opponent's strengths and weaknesses. Read the original (by coach Cicelia Prudent).
